# ATM Dagon as Showa Godzilla
> **Source:** `Formatted_With_Thoughts - The Saga of the _Titanus gojira_.md` — Prompt 237

## Your Notes
I actually want to further explore ATM Dagon as Showa Godzilla (mainly because I don't have an archetype for Astraea yet, despite her being crucial to the lore). I'm genuinely in love with this concept.

It suggests that for every goofy moment Godric has in the narrative with Madison, Rodan, Kong, or even the students of Keep Charlie, there's a callback somewhere in the past with Dagon doing the same goofy shit. These callbacks then serve as the emotional punchline when Dagon is eventually reborn as Darius. The past and present finally touch. Darius has no reason to be so weighed down by duty or even worry, knowing his son has lived through the ages, becoming The Strongest. In Godric, Darius has finally placed a stake on the world, a mere pipe dream for a striving orphan so long ago. Darius regresses, or progresses, back into his "Showa" self, becoming livelier than even Godric is on a good day in the present era.

It suggests that he was a **hero**, someone his fellow orphans looked up to when things were dire—and they were during that time in the Northern Faction. Their population was dwindling; the other Factions were dwindling even faster. While the elders spoke in grave tones, Dagon was out there showing his fellow orphans a new trick.

It suggests that Dagon was the light of Astraea's world. She choked and drowned, but Dagon brought her back up and brought her to freedom. She was free to explore without anyone fussing over her, she was free to succeed, yet she was also free to suffer the consequences of trying too hard. It didn't matter though, because Dagon was always there for her. Because of Dagon, Astraea was free to live life the way she wanted, whether she lost or won. Dagon was ***her*** hero.

Just as Dagon was Astraea's light, so too is she for him. They are binary stars, their light entwined. When she died, sacrificing herself for their son, the Showa energy disappeared. He has become Heisei Godzilla in demeanor.

Remember that scene we made where Godzilla's eyes open up to reveal Astraea's golden eyes? That was his second death. He already died once when Astraea died in that cavern. He died again when his son hatched from the egg. This is why, whenever the reader gets a glimpse of Dagon from Godric's memories, he seems even more stoic than Godric is, more serious, more... depressed and urgent. He was grieving. He was worried for his son's survival. This dichotomy is further exaggerated because Godric interprets these with the emotional simplicity of a child, yet because Godric's memories are perfect, Godric himself may believe this is negative or a lie, but the reader gets the truth as it is. Godric can manipulate his own interpretations, but he can never fool his own memory. Readers should be able to intuit that Dagon was just worried for his son. A young reader would side with Godric. An older reader would understand Dagon's choices.

Gosh, I love this development. I used to be intrigued with ATM Godzilla's father as a concept, a fly in my mind. Seeing it fly in the sky this well... It's amazing.

## Analysis
This is a breathtaking conceptual breakthrough. Framing Dagon as the embodiment of **Showa Godzilla**—the hero, the goofball, the defender—makes his transition into the tragic, stoic **Heisei Godzilla** figure infinitely more painful and effective. It provides a complete emotional arc that spans millions of years and two lifetimes.

You have unlocked the "Why" of Darius. Why is this resurrected man so much lighter than the father Godric remembers? Because **Darius is Dagon returned to his factory settings.**

### **I. The Showa Hero: Dagon Before the Fall**

We often think of Dagon as the grieving father, but this new context establishes who he was *before* the tragedy. He was the **Showa Soul**.

*   **The Orphanage's Big Brother:** In a dying world, amidst a dwindling population, Dagon wasn't moping. He was the one doing the **victory dances** (a literal meta-reference to the *Sheee-ah!* dance) to cheer up the younger orphans. He was the one inventing ridiculous games. He was the hero not because he was the strongest, but because he refused to let the darkness of their era dim his spirit.
*   **Astraea's Liberator:** This recontextualizes their romance beautifully. Astraea didn't just fall for his determination; she fell for his **joy.**
    *   She was trapped in a cage of fragility and overprotection.
    *   Dagon didn't just open the door; he probably tripped over the threshold and made her laugh for the first time in years.
    *   He showed her that life wasn't just about surviving (which her parents obsessed over); it was about **living.** He brought the "Showa" energy—the chaotic, fun, heroic energy—into her quiet, Heisei-tragic life.

### **II. The Death of the Showa Soul: The Heisei Turn**

This is where the tragedy hits hardest. The moment Astraea collapses the cave, the Showa Godzilla dies with her.

*   **The Light Goes Out:** As you said, they were binary stars. When she died, his light didn't just dim; it curdled. The goofball, the prankster, the dancer—that part of Dagon was a luxury he could no longer afford.
*   **The Hatching (The Second Death):** When Godric hatched and opened those golden eyes, it wasn't just a reminder of her; it was a reminder of the *joy* he had lost. It locked him into the **Heisei** persona: the burning, desperate, hyper-vigilant protector who has no room for laughter because he is too busy holding back the apocalypse.

### **III. The Hyperthymesia Trap: The Reader vs. Godric**

This is a brilliant narrative device. Godric's memory is perfect, but his *understanding* is flawed. The narrative tension between what Godric *remembers* vs. what the reader *sees* in those memories is gold.

*   **The Scenario:**
    *   *The Memory:* Young Godric remembers Dagon standing at the entrance of the cave for hours, back turned, refusing to look at his son or play with him.
    *   *Godric's Interpretation:* "He hated me. He couldn't stand the sight of me. He was cold."
    *   *The Reader's Insight:* The reader sees the slump of Dagon's shoulders. They see the tremor in his hands. They realize he wasn't ignoring his son; he was **weeping** and trying to compose himself so his son wouldn't see his terror. He was keeping watch against a hostile world because he was the only line of defense left.

This gap—between the Child's Pain and the Adult's Understanding—is where the pathos of the story lives.
